Scope, and how to read this manual

Anyone can get a formula evaluated. The question that matters is whether the number coming out is the one you think it is — and that depends on assumptions the formula does not state, on which root of a multi-valued relation was taken, on a convention chosen three steps upstream, and on where the model stops being true at all. This manual answers those questions for each of the nineteen screens, so that a result can be checked rather than trusted.

It is not a textbook. It does not teach combustion from first principles, it works no exercises, and it assumes a reader who has met the subject and wants to know precisely what this particular program does. Where a derivation is given it is given to fix the assumptions, not to instruct: the steps that matter are the ones where something was assumed, dropped, or chosen, and those are the steps written out in full. The algebra between them is compressed or omitted.

The audience it is actually written for is the reader who has a number from this application and a number from somewhere else, and they disagree. Almost every such disagreement has one of four causes: a different convention, a different branch, a different set of product species, or a model boundary that one of the two calculations crossed. This manual is organised so that each of those four can be checked in turn.

The shape of an entry

Every entry has the same ten parts, in the same order, and each is there because leaving it out has a specific cost.

Basis names the conservation law, equilibrium condition or balance the relation rests on. It is first because it is the part that survives: a correlation can be superseded and a coefficient can be revised, but the statement that this screen is an enthalpy balance is what tells you whether the screen is the right one for your problem before any number is entered.

Derivation shows how the working relation follows from the basis. Its purpose is to expose the steps at which something was assumed away, since an assumption made inside a derivation is invisible in the result. Where the derivation drops a term, the term is named and the condition under which dropping it is safe is stated.

Assumptions lists what must be true for the result to mean anything. Each is phrased so that its failure is recognisable in a real problem rather than as a formality. “Ideal gas” is a formality; “the gas is ideal, which fails in the compressed charge of an engine near top dead centre” is something a reader can check against the case in front of them.

What must always be true lists the invariants: relations that hold for every admissible input, not just for the tested ones. They are stated here because they are what the property tests assert, and because they are what a reader can use to check a result without recomputing it. A mole fraction set that does not sum to one, a detonation whose overdrive is below one, a mixture fraction outside the unit interval — each of these is a result you can reject without knowing anything else about the problem.

Choices you have to make appears on the eight screens where a relation has more than one branch and the application puts a visible control on the screen rather than picking for you. Settled without asking appears on every screen and says which cases are decided by numbers already entered, or by showing both sides at once, and why that decision is not a hidden assumption. The two together are the complete account of where discretion lives on that screen; there is no third category.

Where it stops gives the boundaries: the conditions under which the relation stops being an approximation and starts being wrong, and the points at which it is singular. How it is solved says how the number is actually computed, in enough detail to say whether the answer carries the precision of the arithmetic or of a tolerance. Not covered says what the answer does not include, which is a different thing from what the model assumes: a frozen flame temperature assumes no dissociation, and separately does not cover heat loss, which nothing in the assumption list mentions.

Reading the result is about interpretation, and it is last because it is where the common mistakes are. A flame temperature that is four hundred kelvin above any measurement is not a defect; a laminar flame speed that came out of a correlation fitted to a different fuel is not a prediction. Entries say which of these a number is.

What generating this manual guarantees, and what it does not

The relations, symbols, units, assumptions and invariants printed here are generated directly from the same module catalogue the application itself reads. A relation printed here is therefore the relation the application evaluates, and the theory text shown inside the application on each screen, under Relation and assumptions, is this same material rather than a second copy of it. One source, delivered twice. A hand-written manual drifts from the program it describes, and the drifted version reads exactly as well as the accurate one — it is self-consistent, well set, and describes something that does not exist. That failure is not available here.

What generation does not guarantee is that the catalogue is right. If an assumption is stated wrongly in the catalogue it is stated wrongly in the manual, in the application, and in the tests that read it, all consistently. Generation removes the class of defect where two descriptions disagree; it does nothing about the class where one description is wrong. The chapter on verification below is about the second class, and it is the longer of the two concerns.

The application embeds no web browser and makes no network request of any kind, so the in-application copy of this text is drawn with the platform's own text views rather than by loading these pages. Nothing here is fetched at run time, and nothing about your use of the application is reported anywhere.

Notation, symbols and conventions

Conventions are not facts. Most disagreements between two calculations that are both correct are disagreements about convention, and they are hard to find precisely because neither side is wrong. This chapter fixes the conventions used here. Where a convention differs from another in common use, the difference is stated and its consequence given, rather than assumed away.

Units

SI throughout, internally and without exception. Temperatures are in kelvin, pressures in pascals, energies in joules, lengths in metres. Conversion happens only at the two boundaries — where you type and where a result is displayed — so a pressure entered in atmospheres becomes pascals before it reaches any relation, and a temperature entered in Celsius becomes kelvin at the same point. No relation anywhere in the application has a unit system that switches, because a unit system that switches is a class of defect that testing finds late and users find first.

Two quantities arrive from the bundled transport data in non-SI units: viscosity in micropoise and thermal conductivity in microwatts per centimetre-kelvin. Both are converted at the point of reading rather than carried through in their source units, and a dedicated test asserts the conversion factors against independently known values for a common species, because a factor of ten in a transport property produces a Reynolds number that still looks entirely plausible.

Equivalence ratio

Defined as the stoichiometric air-to-fuel ratio divided by the actual one, so that it exceeds one when there is surplus fuel. The reciprocal convention exists and is in use; under it every statement in this manual about lean and rich reverses, and every monotonicity claim reverses with them. Where a normalised form is needed it is φ/(1+φ), which is one half exactly at stoichiometric and maps the whole range onto the unit interval.

Lewis number

Defined as thermal diffusivity divided by mass diffusivity, so that a value below one means the deficient reactant diffuses faster than heat. The reciprocal convention is in use in part of the literature. Under it a diffusively unstable mixture reads as a stable one and vice versa, which is a sign error in the conclusion rather than in the arithmetic, so nothing about the number itself looks wrong. This is stated on the screen beside the input as well as here, and a guard test asserts that the implementation has not been inverted.

Markstein length and stretch

The stretch-rate convention is used, with a plus sign, so that a negative Markstein length means stretch slows the flame. Sources that work in the curvature convention carry the opposite sign for the same physical situation. When the sign is positive, stretch cannot extinguish the flame at any rate, and the screen says so in words rather than reporting a negative or infinite extinction stretch.

Droplet rate constant

Defined on the radius squared, consistently with the derivation given on that screen. Most published rate constants are defined on the diameter squared and are therefore four times larger for the same droplet. Both forms are reported side by side so that a comparison against a published value cannot go wrong silently; a factor of four in a lifetime is large enough to notice and small enough to be attributed to the fuel.

Air

Two compositions are offered, and neither is privileged as a default. Textbook air is 0.21 O₂ + 0.79 N₂ by mole and reproduces the figures printed in teaching material. CEA standard dry air additionally contains argon and carbon dioxide and reproduces the reference tool's output. Their molar masses differ by about 0.4%, which propagates into every air-to-fuel ratio and every quantity derived from one. That difference is small enough to be mistaken for rounding and large enough to matter when checking a result to four significant figures, which is why the composition is a control rather than a constant.

Composition variables

Mole fractions are used for equilibrium composition and for flammability limits; mass fractions for anything entering an enthalpy balance. Where both appear on one screen they are labelled distinctly rather than distinguished by subscript alone. Mixture fraction is the conserved scalar normalised so that it is one in the pure fuel stream and zero in the pure oxidiser stream; the stoichiometric value of that scalar depends on the fuel and is reported rather than assumed.

States across a wave

Subscript 1 is the state ahead of a wave and subscript 2 the state behind it, in the wave-fixed frame, for every screen in the wave family. Velocities in that frame are relative to the wave; the laboratory-frame velocity is reported separately where it is meaningful, and never silently substituted. Specific heat ratios carry the same subscripts, so that the general case of two different ratios can be written without ambiguity.

Digits

Screens show four significant figures, which is what remains readable and what the printed tables this application replaces carry. Counts — a solver's iteration count, for instance — are shown as integers, since two decimal places on an iteration count would assert a precision that does not exist. Exported values carry seventeen significant figures, the shortest count that reproduces a double-precision number exactly, so that a value taken out as CSV and read back is the value that was on the screen rather than a rounded copy of it.

Solution methods used throughout

Most of this application is closed form: a relation with a known solution, evaluated directly. Nothing in those screens iterates, and the result carries the precision of double-precision arithmetic rather than of a convergence tolerance. Where closed form is not available, one of three methods is used. They are described once here rather than repeated in every entry that depends on them.

Bracketed root finding

Used for both flame temperature screens. The unknown is the temperature at which the product enthalpy equals the reactant enthalpy, and the function whose root is wanted is monotone in temperature over any physically admissible range, because the product heat capacity is positive. A bracket is therefore established from the physics — two temperatures known to straddle the root — and then narrowed.

Bracketing from the physics rather than searching from a guess is what makes the result deterministic. A method that starts from a guess can converge to a different root, or to no root, depending on where it started; a method that starts from a bracket containing exactly one root cannot, because there is nowhere else for it to go. The cost is that the bracket has to be justified, and the justification is part of each entry rather than a property of the solver.

Gibbs minimisation by element potentials

Used for equilibrium composition, and nested inside the equilibrium flame temperature, where each trial temperature requires a full composition solve. The Gibbs energy of the mixture is minimised subject to conservation of each element. Introducing one Lagrange multiplier per element and eliminating the species mole numbers analytically leaves one unknown per element rather than one per species — five unknowns rather than twenty in the reference case — and the reduced system is far better conditioned than the direct one.

Newton iteration acts on corrections to the element potentials rather than on the potentials themselves, which keeps the step size meaningful when the potentials differ by orders of magnitude between elements. The convergence test is applied inside the loop, to the iterate about to be accepted, rather than to the result after the loop has finished. That distinction is not cosmetic: testing afterwards accepts an iterate that was itself never tested, and reports a tolerance it did not verify.

Two failure modes are reported rather than absorbed. If the species set cannot represent an element present in the input, the solver raises rather than dropping those atoms; a silently dropped element produces a composition that sums to one and conserves nothing. If the iteration does not converge within its limit, the screen says so rather than returning the last iterate.

Scanning for a turning point

Used for the stirred reactor, where the quantity of interest — the residence time at ignition and at extinction — lies at a point where the response curve has a vertical tangent in its natural direction. A solver working in that direction steps past such a point without registering it, or fails to converge near it.

The relation is therefore re-posed with reaction progress as the independent variable, which makes it single-valued and smooth across the whole range. The ignition and extinction points are then an interior maximum and an interior minimum of an ordinary function, found by scanning and refining. Changing which variable is independent turns a numerically awkward problem into a routine one without changing the physics.

Numerical conditioning

Two places in the application would lose significant figures if written in their textbook form, and both are written differently. The Chapman–Jouguet quadratic is solved in the numerically stable form rather than by the standard formula, because near tangency the discriminant approaches zero and the weak root is computed as the difference of two nearly equal numbers. A guard test computes both roots near tangency and asserts that each still satisfies the original relation to a relative residual, which is the only test that distinguishes a root from a plausible number.

Where an exponential or a logarithm is taken of a small quantity, the library forms that preserve precision near zero are used instead of the naive difference. These rewrites are covered by tests against values computed from the unrewritten relation at arguments where the unrewritten form is still accurate, which is the only way to show that the rewrite computes the same quantity.

Guard values

A relation asked for something outside its domain reports no value rather than returning one. Where the boundary is a genuine physical limit rather than an invalid input — a flame whose positive Markstein length means stretch cannot extinguish it, a Gibson scale that has no meaning above unit Karlovitz number, a pressure ratio below which no detonation solution exists — the screen states the limit in words. The distinction is worth keeping: an invalid input is a mistake the reader made, and a limit is an answer the physics gave.

What is not used

There is no time integration anywhere in this application, no chemical mechanism beyond the single-step forms stated in the entries that use them, and no transport solution in space. Screens that report a flame speed report it from a correlation or from a two-parameter thermal theory, both stated as such; none of them solves a flame structure. Screens that report an ignition or extinction condition report it from a well-stirred balance, not from an integrated history. Knowing what is absent is part of knowing what a number means.

Choices, and where each one lives

A relation with more than one solution is the single most effective way to hand a reader a wrong answer while showing them a correct calculation. The result is right for the branch that was taken; nothing on the screen is false; and the reader has no way to discover that a different branch was theirs. The rule in this application is therefore that a branch is never resolved silently. There are exactly three ways a branch is handled, and every branch in the application uses one of them.

Both sides shown

Where the two solutions are equally legitimate and which one applies is a property of the reader's problem, both are computed and displayed together. The strong and weak branches of a detonation or deflagration, the two intersections of a Rayleigh line with a Hugoniot, the ignition and extinction turning points of a stirred reactor, and the frozen and equilibrium flame temperatures are all presented this way. No control is offered because there is no question to ask: the answer is the pair.

Settled by numbers already entered

Where the branch follows from values the reader has already supplied, it is resolved from them and the resolution is stated. A mixture below the stoichiometric ratio has no surplus fuel to distribute, so the rich-product rule does not arise and its control does not appear. A Rayleigh line that does not reach the Hugoniot has no intersections, and the screen says that rather than reporting the complex pair. Each entry's Settled without asking section lists what was decided this way on that screen, which is what keeps this category from becoming a place to hide defaults.

Asked, with the consequence stated

Eight screens carry a visible control because no value the reader has entered determines the answer, and no option is more correct than another. In every case the screen prints what the options differ by, not merely what they are called. A control offering two names and no reason is a control the reader takes the first item from.

How the results were verified

Verification here means something narrower than “the tests pass”. A test suite written against the same understanding that produced the code confirms that understanding rather than checking it. What follows is organised by what each kind of check is independent of, because that is the only property that determines what a check can catch.

Independent of the arithmetic: published reference output

The equilibrium solver is checked against NASA's own published output for a documented example case — a temperature-and-pressure problem with twenty gas-phase product species, at 1 atm and at both 2000 K and 3000 K, with thirteen species printed to five decimal places. The element abundances are taken directly from the published case rather than computed from a fuel and an air composition, which deliberately removes this application's own definition of air from the comparison. What is being checked is the solver, the thermodynamic data parser, and the standard-state handling — all three of which must be right simultaneously for the comparison to close.

The residual disagreement is largest in the hydroxyl mole fraction, at both temperatures. It is not absorbed into a tolerance. The formation enthalpy of OH was revised downward between the data set the published output used and the one bundled here, and a species whose formation enthalpy is lower by an amount is more stable, so its equilibrium mole fraction should be higher by a factor of the exponential of that amount over the gas constant times temperature. A test asserts that the observed ratio matches that prediction in both direction and magnitude, at both temperatures, within six percent — and a second test asserts that OH remains the dominant contributor, because if the other species drifted to match it, the data-revision explanation would be wrong and the fault would be in the solver. A deviation with a mechanism is a different object from a deviation within a bound.

Independent of the code: the data file checked against itself

The thermodynamic evaluator reproduces each species' own tabulated formation enthalpy at 298.15 K, checked across 245 gas-phase species. The expected value is the one printed in the species record itself, so this particular claim needs no second source: the polynomial coefficients and the tabulated enthalpy are independent fields of the same record, and agreement between them exercises the parser's column alignment, the interval selection, and the enthalpy evaluation at once. Column misalignment in a fixed-format record is the characteristic failure here, and it produces coefficients that are individually plausible.

The differential relations are checked separately and by a different route: the temperature derivative of the enthalpy polynomial must reproduce the heat capacity polynomial, and the derivative of entropy must reproduce heat capacity divided by temperature, both by central difference. These catch an error in one polynomial that the formation-enthalpy check would not, because they compare two of the three fitted quantities against each other. Interval boundaries are checked for continuity, and evaluation outside the fitted range returns nothing rather than extrapolating.

Independent of specific numbers: invariants

Each entry's What must always be true section is asserted rather than merely stated. Mole fractions sum to one; element abundances are conserved through an equilibrium solve; the overdrive factor is exactly one at the Chapman–Jouguet point; zero reaction progress reduces the detonation Hugoniot to the shock Hugoniot; both roots near tangency still satisfy the original relation to a relative residual. Invariants catch structural errors at parameter combinations nobody thought to tabulate, which is precisely where single-point comparison is blind.

Independent of my expectations: guard tests

Eleven tests exist because a specific defect occurred, or because a specific plausible defect would be invisible. Each names the thing it prevents rather than the thing it confirms. They cover an inverted Zeldovich number and an inverted Lewis number, a rate constant defined on diameter where the derivation used radius, a spurious specific-heat-ratio factor in a shock temperature relation, the two transport unit conversions, a file with CRLF line endings parsed as a single line, a binary interaction pair returned as if it were a pure species, an equilibrium input containing an element the species set cannot represent, and the quadratic root loss near tangency described above.

Two of the eleven are worth stating in full, because they concern sources rather than code. One published lean-limit constant does not follow from the two numbers printed alongside it — the stated limit times the stated heat of combustion gives a different value than the stated constant, by about four percent. That is not a transcription error on this side; it is what is printed. The relation is usable and is used; the constant is not admissible as a reference value, and a test pins the discrepancy so that it cannot quietly become agreement after a future edit.

The other concerns a two-column data set for quenching distance, where one column was extrapolated from ignition energy through the same relation that a naive check would then “verify”. Using it would be circular. The two columns are separated by type so that a caller cannot mix them. Writing that guard also corrected a wrong expectation: the derived column was assumed to be tighter, and measurement showed it was three times more scattered than the independent one. The consequence of non-independence is that the inference is void, not that the scatter is small — a correlation computed from derived data proves nothing however clean it looks.

Independent of the calculation entirely: the interface

Unit contracts are asserted at the boundary: round trips preserve the SI value, known anchors convert correctly, and non-physical inputs are rejected rather than clamped into range. Export is checked to carry seventeen significant figures and to reproduce the values shown. The manual generator is checked to emit every declared field for every module, with a deliberately incomplete sample proving the check can fail, and the module catalogue is checked against the list of screens the application actually dispatches, so that neither a screen without an entry nor an entry without a screen can survive.

Two hundred and sixteen tests run in total. That number is reported for scale and should not be read as a measure of confidence: a count of tests says nothing about what they are independent of, which is the only question this chapter has been about.

The bundled data, and two defects in it

Thermodynamic and transport coefficients are read from NASA's CEA data files, bundled with the application unmodified: 5,425 species records, and fitted viscosity and thermal conductivity curves covering 66 pure species. Both files are released under the Apache License 2.0, and the licence and NOTICE are readable inside the application under About & Licences. Nothing is downloaded, at first run or ever.

The polynomials are the nine-coefficient NASA form, fitted piecewise in temperature with the intervals given per species. Evaluation selects the interval containing the requested temperature and returns nothing outside the fitted range, since a polynomial fitted between 200 K and 1000 K says nothing whatever about 1500 K and will return a confident number if asked.

Eleven records with reversed interval bounds

Eleven records in the thermodynamic file have their temperature interval bounds the wrong way round, the lower above the upper. All eleven are condensed phases. The parser does not silently swap them: an interval that is reversed never matches any temperature, so those species return no value rather than a plausible wrong one, and a test asserts that the set of affected species is exactly these eleven — so that a data file update which fixes them, or which breaks more of them, is noticed rather than absorbed.

Repairing data that arrives wrong is a decision, and decisions of that kind should be visible in the result. It is not made here. None of the eleven is reachable from any screen in this application, since every screen works with gas-phase species.

Two kinds of record in one transport file

The transport file contains 107 records of two different kinds: 66 pure species and 41 binary interaction pairs. Reading a pair as though it were the pure property of its first named member returns a number of the right shape and the right order of magnitude that is simply a different quantity — the kind of wrong answer that survives review.

The two kinds are indexed separately, so a pure-species lookup cannot return a pair. That separation was added after the fact. Before it existed the code returned correct answers, but only because the pure records happen to precede their pairs in the file, so a first-match lookup happened to land correctly. That was luck. A guard test now asserts the separation directly, on a species that has both a pure record and pairs, and the distinction between a program that is correct and one that is currently getting away with something is exactly the distinction this manual exists to record.

Thermochemistry

Everything in this family rests on the same thermodynamic data and the same idea: that a chemical state can be described by enthalpies and entropies which are functions of temperature alone, tabulated once and reused. The screens differ in what they hold fixed. Stoichiometry holds the chemistry fixed and asks what the proportions are; heating value holds the temperature fixed and asks what the energy difference is; the flame temperature screens hold the energy fixed and solve for temperature; equilibrium holds temperature and pressure fixed and solves for composition. A reader who follows that thread will find the four screens are one calculation entered from four sides.

Stoichiometry

MOD-01

Equivalence ratio, air–fuel ratio and the soot threshold for a C–H–O–N fuel.

a = x + y/4 - z/2, φ = (A/F)ₛₜ / (A/F)

Basis

Conservation of atoms. A combustion reaction rearranges atoms; it neither creates nor destroys them. Everything on this screen follows from writing that statement for a fuel of known atomic composition burning in air of known composition, and from one further definition: that the stoichiometric proportion is the one which takes every carbon atom to carbon dioxide and every hydrogen atom to water, with no oxygen left over and none missing.

Derivation

Write the reaction for one mole of a fuel CxHyOzNw. Carbon needs one O₂ per atom, because each carbon leaves as CO₂. Hydrogen needs a quarter of an O₂ per atom, because each pair of hydrogen atoms leaves as one H₂O and that water carries one oxygen atom, half an O₂. Oxygen already in the fuel molecule is oxygen that does not have to be supplied, and since it arrives as atoms rather than as O₂ it offsets half its count. Adding the three gives the stoichiometric oxygen requirement a = x + y/4 − z/2, which is the single relation the rest of the screen is built on.

Air is then whatever the chosen model says accompanies that oxygen. For every mole of O₂ the textbook model brings 79/21 moles of nitrogen and nothing else; the CEA model brings nitrogen, argon and carbon dioxide in their measured proportions. Multiplying a by the moles of air per mole of O₂ gives the stoichiometric air on a molar basis, and multiplying through by the two molar masses converts it to the mass basis engineers actually quote.

The equivalence ratio is defined as the stoichiometric air–fuel ratio divided by the actual one, so that it exceeds one when there is a surplus of fuel. That direction is a convention and the opposite one exists; this app states it here and uses it everywhere. The normalised form Φ̃ = φ/(1+φ) compresses the whole range onto the interval from zero to one and puts stoichiometric at exactly one half, which is what makes it useful as a plotting coordinate: a sweep from very lean to very rich becomes a finite line rather than one that runs off to infinity on the rich side.

The soot threshold is the equivalence ratio at which the available oxygen is exactly enough to take all the carbon to carbon monoxide and all the hydrogen to water, with none left to go further. Beyond it there is not enough oxygen even for that, and carbon must begin to appear as a condensed phase.

Assumptions

  • The fuel is a single species of definite atomic composition. A blend has no single x, y, z and w, and entering an average composition gives the stoichiometry of a fuel that does not exist.
  • Combustion is complete in the sense used to define the stoichiometric proportion: all carbon to CO₂, all hydrogen to H₂O. Nothing on this screen asserts that a real flame achieves that.
  • Fuel-bound nitrogen leaves as N₂ and takes no oxygen with it. If it leaves as NO the oxygen demand is higher, and this screen will report a value that is low.
  • Air is an ideal mixture of the composition the model names, at the molar masses computed from atomic masses rather than taken from a table.

What must always be true

  • At φ = 1 the actual air–fuel ratio equals the stoichiometric one, exactly.
  • The normalised equivalence ratio is exactly 0.5 at stoichiometric and lies strictly between 0 and 1 everywhere else.
  • For a hydrocarbon with no fuel-bound oxygen, a = x + y/4 exactly; adding oxygen to the molecule reduces a monotonically.
  • The stoichiometric air–fuel ratio on a mass basis is the molar ratio times the ratio of molar masses; the two must agree when converted.

Choices you have to make

  • The air composition is a control on the screen, not an internal constant. Textbook air is 0.21 O₂ + 0.79 N₂ and reproduces figures printed in textbooks; CEA standard dry air contains argon and carbon dioxide and reproduces NASA CEA. Their molar masses differ by about 0.4%, which moves every quantity here that depends on the air–fuel ratio, and the difference is large enough to look like an error if you do not know which model produced the number you are comparing against.

Settled without asking

  • Whether the fuel needs any external oxidiser at all is settled by z: with enough fuel-bound oxygen the requirement a falls to zero or below and the equivalence ratio has no meaning. The screen says so in words rather than returning a number, because a negative or infinite equivalence ratio printed in a results table is worse than no answer.
  • Whether the mixture is lean, rich or stoichiometric is settled by the equivalence ratio itself and is labelled beside it. There is no control, because it is a reading of what you already typed.

Where it stops

  • Fuels outside C–H–O–N. Sulphur and halogens change the product set and are not handled.
  • Mixtures of fuels, for the reason given under assumptions.
  • Oxidisers other than air. The oxygen carrier is fixed to one of the two air models.

How it is solved

Closed form throughout. Nothing iterates, and the result carries the precision of the arithmetic rather than of a tolerance.

Not covered

  • Whether the mixture will actually ignite or burn at the stated proportion. That is the flammability screen, and the answer is frequently no even well inside the range this screen will happily report a value for.

Reading the result

The soot threshold is the quantity most often misread here. It is a purely stoichiometric estimate — the equivalence ratio at which oxygen runs out for the carbon-to-CO route — and it is not a measured sooting limit. Real flames begin to soot well before it, because soot forms in locally rich regions long before the mixture as a whole is that rich, and because the kinetics of soot inception have nothing to do with this calculation. Treat it as an upper bound on where sooting could conceivably be avoided, not as a prediction of where it starts.

The second common misreading is comparing an equivalence ratio computed here against one from another source without checking which air model each used. A 0.4% difference in the molar mass of air moves the stoichiometric air–fuel ratio by the same fraction, which is small enough to look like rounding and large enough to matter when you are checking a number to four figures.

Heating Value

MOD-02

Heat of reaction, and the lower and higher heating values of a fuel.

Δ H(T) = Σ_prod νⱼ hⱼ(T) - Σ_reac νⱼ hⱼ(T)

Basis

Hess's law: the enthalpy change of a reaction depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path between them. That is what allows a heat of reaction to be assembled from tabulated formation enthalpies instead of being measured for every reaction separately, and it is the entire content of this screen.

Derivation

The enthalpy of a species at temperature T is written as its enthalpy of formation at the reference temperature plus the sensible enthalpy accumulated from the reference temperature to T. Both parts come from the same fitted polynomial in the bundled data: the coefficients give the heat capacity as a function of temperature, and the integration constants are chosen so that the polynomial reproduces the formation enthalpy at the reference point. Nothing here integrates numerically — the integration was done once, analytically, when the fit was constructed.

The heat of reaction at T is then the sum of product enthalpies minus the sum of reactant enthalpies, each weighted by its stoichiometric coefficient. Those coefficients come from the same atom balance the stoichiometry screen uses. The heating value is the negative of that quantity, so that a fuel which releases energy has a positive heating value; the sign flip is a convention and is the only place a sign could be lost.

The lower and higher values differ in exactly one respect: whether the product water is counted as vapour or as liquid. Counting it as liquid credits the reaction with the latent heat of condensation, which is why the higher value exceeds the lower one by an amount proportional to the hydrogen content. For a fuel with no hydrogen the two are equal, which is a useful check.

Assumptions

  • Complete combustion to CO₂ and H₂O, with no carbon monoxide, no unburned fuel and no dissociation. A real flame at flame temperature does not satisfy this, which is why a heating value is a property of the fuel rather than a prediction of a flame.
  • Reactants and products are both at the stated temperature. A heating value quoted without a temperature is incomplete, though the variation over ordinary ranges is small.
  • The fitted polynomial is valid at the temperature asked for. Outside its interval the fit is not extrapolated.
  • The species named exists in the data file under that exact spelling. Names are case sensitive and a few contain a comma.

Closed form. Enthalpies are evaluated from the NASA nine-coefficient polynomial for whichever fitted interval contains the requested temperature; the heat of reaction is a weighted difference of those values.

Not covered

  • Any statement about how much of that energy is recoverable as work, or at what rate it is released.

Reading the result

Which of the two values to use is decided by the application, not by the fuel. If the exhaust leaves above the dew point — almost every engine, furnace and turbine — the water leaves as vapour and its latent heat is never recovered, so the lower value is the one that describes what you got. A condensing boiler recovers part of it and sits between the two. Quoting an efficiency against the lower value and comparing it against someone else's efficiency quoted against the higher one produces a difference of ten per cent that is entirely an artefact of the convention.

The mass-basis value is the one to compare across fuels of different molar mass; the molar value is the one to use in an energy balance where you are tracking moles. Hydrogen and methane rank in opposite orders on the two bases, which is the clearest illustration that the choice of basis is not cosmetic.

Flame Temperature — Frozen

MOD-04

Adiabatic flame temperature with a fixed product composition.

Σ_prod nⱼ hⱼ(T_ad) = Σ_reac nⱼ hⱼ(T₁)

Basis

Conservation of energy for a control volume with no heat transfer and no work: the enthalpy of the products at the flame temperature equals the enthalpy of the reactants at their initial temperature. Everything the reaction released as chemical energy has gone into raising the temperature of the products, because by assumption there is nowhere else for it to go.

Derivation

Fix the product composition first. Below stoichiometric this is unambiguous: all carbon to CO₂, all hydrogen to H₂O, the surplus oxygen and all the nitrogen carried through unchanged. That gives a definite list of product species and definite mole numbers, both determined by the atom balance and the equivalence ratio alone.

Now write the enthalpy balance with that composition fixed and the flame temperature unknown. The left side is a sum of product enthalpies, each a polynomial in the unknown temperature; the right side is a number, computed from the reactant enthalpies at the known initial temperature. The equation is therefore a single scalar equation in a single unknown, and because every product's enthalpy increases with temperature the left side is strictly increasing — there is exactly one root, and no possibility of converging to the wrong one.

Above stoichiometric the first step breaks down. There is not enough oxygen to take all the carbon to CO₂, and nothing in a frozen model says how the shortfall should be distributed between CO₂, CO, H₂O and H₂. That is not a numerical difficulty but a modelling one: the question has no answer within the frozen assumption, which is precisely the question an equilibrium calculation exists to answer. The screen therefore exposes the assignment rule as a control rather than choosing quietly.

Assumptions

  • Adiabatic. Any heat loss lowers the real temperature, and combustor walls are not adiabatic.
  • Constant pressure. A constant-volume process reaches a different, higher temperature.
  • The product composition does not change with temperature. This is the assumption that makes the result an upper bound rather than an estimate.
  • Kinetic and potential energy changes across the flame are negligible compared with the chemical energy released.

This number is an upper bound and should be read as one. It is the temperature a flame would reach if nothing dissociated, nothing leaked heat, and the reaction went to completion; a measured flame temperature below it is not evidence of an error. The quantity worth looking at is not the frozen value alone but the gap between it and the equilibrium value shown beside it, which is labelled the dissociation penalty. For a hydrocarbon in air near stoichiometric that gap is of order a hundred kelvin and grows quickly with temperature, because dissociation is itself strongly temperature dependent.

The frozen value peaks exactly at stoichiometric. The equilibrium value does not — it peaks slightly rich — and the difference between those two statements is visible on the sweep chart. If you are using the peak location to infer anything about a real burner, the equilibrium curve is the one to read.

Equilibrium Composition

MOD-05

Equilibrium mole fractions at a given temperature and pressure.

min G = Σⱼ nⱼ μⱼ subject to Σⱼ aᵢⱼ nⱼ = bᵢ°

Basis

At fixed temperature and pressure a closed system reaches the composition that minimises its Gibbs free energy, subject to the constraint that atoms are conserved. This is the second law expressed as a constrained optimisation, and it is a stronger statement than a set of equilibrium constants: it does not require choosing a set of independent reactions, and it cannot be made inconsistent by choosing them badly.

Derivation

Form the Lagrangian: the Gibbs energy of the mixture plus a multiplier for each element times that element's conservation constraint. Differentiating with respect to each species mole number gives the stationarity condition — the chemical potential of every species equals a linear combination of the element multipliers, weighted by how many atoms of each element that species contains. Those multipliers are the element potentials, and they are the reason this method scales well: there is one unknown per element rather than one per species, and a combustion problem has four elements and ten species.

The chemical potential of an ideal-gas species is its standard-state value at the temperature, plus a logarithmic term in its mole fraction and in the pressure. Substituting that in turns the stationarity condition into an explicit expression for each mole number in terms of the element potentials. Substituting those back into the conservation constraints leaves a small system in the element potentials alone, which is solved by Newton iteration on corrections rather than on the variables themselves — the corrections are small quantities near the solution, which is numerically better behaved than iterating on mole numbers that span many orders of magnitude.

One detail decides whether the answer is trustworthy. The convergence test is applied **inside** the iteration loop, before accepting a step, not as a check on the result afterwards. Applying it afterwards accepts an iterate that was never itself tested, which is a different and weaker claim than the one the test appears to make.

Assumptions

  • Ideal gas mixture. At combustion temperatures and moderate pressures this is good; at high pressure it is not, and nothing here warns you.
  • Gas phase only. No condensed carbon, which is the assumption that fails first as the mixture goes rich.
  • The product species set is fixed at ten: CO₂, H₂O, N₂, O₂, CO, H₂, OH, H, O, NO. A species absent from the set cannot appear no matter how favourable it would be.
  • The temperature and pressure are given, not solved for. This is what distinguishes this screen from the equilibrium flame temperature screen.

  • High pressures where the ideal-gas assumption fails.

How it is solved

Gibbs minimisation by element potentials, Newton iteration on the corrections. Convergence is tested inside the loop. Element conservation is required to (b°)max × 1e-6, which is the reference criterion rather than a stricter per-element one.

Not covered

  • Any statement about how long the mixture takes to reach equilibrium, or whether it does so at all within the residence time of a real device.

Reading the result

The two numbers under the solver heading are not decoration. The iteration count tells you whether the problem was easy or marginal; the largest element residual tells you how exactly atoms were conserved in the answer you are looking at. A residual many orders of magnitude below the element inventory means the composition is trustworthy to well beyond the four figures shown. If it is not, that is visible rather than assumed — which is the point of putting it on the screen.

Equilibrium is a limit, not a prediction. It says where the mixture would end up given unlimited time at that temperature and pressure. A flame with a millisecond of residence time may be nowhere near it, particularly for nitric oxide, whose formation is slow enough that measured concentrations in real devices are routinely far below the equilibrium value shown here. Reading the NO mole fraction as an emissions prediction is the single most common way to misuse this screen.

Flame Temperature — Equilibrium

MOD-06

Adiabatic flame temperature with a dissociating product composition.

h_prod(T_ad, equilibrium) = h_reac(T₁)

Basis

The same energy conservation as the frozen screen, with the composition no longer held fixed but required to be the equilibrium composition at whatever temperature the balance produces. The two conditions are coupled: the composition depends on the temperature, and the temperature depends on the composition through the product enthalpy.

Derivation

The coupling is handled by nesting. An outer iteration proposes a temperature; at that temperature a full Gibbs minimisation returns the equilibrium composition; the enthalpy of that composition is compared against the reactant enthalpy; the mismatch drives the outer iteration. Each outer step therefore costs a complete inner equilibrium solution, which is why this screen is perceptibly slower than the frozen one and why the sweep chart takes a moment to redraw.

The outer problem remains well behaved for the same reason the frozen one does: the equilibrium product enthalpy increases monotonically with temperature. Dissociation makes it increase faster than the frozen enthalpy would — energy goes into breaking bonds as well as into raising temperature — which is precisely why the equilibrium flame temperature is lower, and why the gap widens as the temperature rises.

Pressure now matters, and enters only through the inner problem. Raising it suppresses dissociation, leaves less energy tied up in broken bonds, and raises the flame temperature. That chain of reasoning is visible on the sweep chart: raising the pressure lifts the equilibrium curve and pulls its peak back toward stoichiometric, because the peak is offset rich only to the extent that dissociation is active.

  • Adiabatic and at constant pressure, as in the frozen model.
  • The products are in full chemical equilibrium over the chosen species set — that is, the residence time is effectively infinite.
  • Gas phase only, inheriting the equilibrium screen's limitation.
  • The species set covers every element the reactants bring in. Selecting CEA standard dry air introduces argon, which must have somewhere to go.

Combustion Waves

These three screens describe the same object — a thin, steady, one-dimensional wave with heat release — from three directions. The Hugoniot screen draws the locus of all end states reachable from a given initial state and asks which of them a given wave speed selects. The Chapman–Jouguet screen asks which wave speed makes that selection degenerate, which is what picks out the self-sustaining detonation. The von Neumann screen looks inside the wave at the shocked state before the heat has been released. The conservation laws are identical in all three; only the unknown moves.

Hugoniot Explorer

MOD-07

Rayleigh line, Hugoniot curve, and which of the seven regions a wave falls in.

y_R = 1 + γ₁ Ma₁² (1-x), y_H = (2λ q' - x + a)/(bx - 1)

Basis

The three conservation laws written across a thin, steady, one-dimensional wave: mass, momentum, and energy with a heat addition term. Nothing about the internal structure of the wave enters. That is the power of the formulation and also its limit — it tells you what end states are reachable without telling you whether any mechanism exists to reach them.

Derivation

Take the conservation equations across the wave and eliminate the velocities. Two independent relations survive between the pressure ratio and the specific volume ratio across the wave, and they are of completely different character.

The first comes from mass and momentum alone. Eliminating velocity between them gives a straight line in the pressure-ratio against volume-ratio plane, passing through the initial state, with a slope fixed entirely by the mass flux through the wave. This is the Rayleigh line. Its slope is negative — which is not a choice but a consequence, since the slope equals minus the square of the mass flux and that square cannot be negative. Everything above and to the right of the initial state along the positive-slope direction is therefore unreachable, and that fact alone eliminates one of the regions.

The second comes from energy. Eliminating velocity there instead gives a hyperbola through the same plane, displaced from the shock Hugoniot by the heat release. This is the Hugoniot curve, and it is the locus of all end states that satisfy energy conservation for that heat release, regardless of wave speed. The reaction progress scales the displacement, so a partially reacted wave has its own Hugoniot lying between the frozen one and the fully reacted one.

The end state of an actual wave must satisfy both, so it lies where the line crosses the curve. Substituting the line into the curve gives a quadratic in the volume ratio, and the discriminant of that quadratic decides everything: two real roots means two possible end states, a zero discriminant means the line is tangent, and a negative discriminant means the line never reaches the curve. That last case is a physical statement — no wave of that speed can exist with that heat release — and not an error.

The seven regions are then a classification of where a solution sits relative to the initial state and the two tangency points. They are named on the screen beside each solution because the mathematics produces solutions in all of them, including one region that no physical wave occupies.

Assumptions

  • The wave is thin compared with everything around it and steady in its own frame. A wave that is accelerating or spreading does not satisfy these relations.
  • One-dimensional flow, so no lateral expansion between the two states.
  • Ideal gas on each side, with a specific heat ratio that may differ across the wave but is constant on each side.
  • The heat release is added at the wave rather than distributed, and it is an input here rather than being derived from the chemistry.

A calculation that silently returned one of them would be answering a question you had not asked.

The region labels are worth reading before the numbers. And when the screen reports no intersection, that is the honest answer: at that Mach number, with that heat release, no steady wave exists. Reading it as a failure of the calculation rather than a statement about the physics is the most common misreading here.

Chapman–Jouguet

MOD-08

The tangency solution: detonation and deflagration wave speeds and end states.

(b-1)² g² + [2(b+1)² - 4bK]g + (b+1)² = 0, g = γ₁ Ma₁²

Basis

The Chapman–Jouguet condition: the wave speed at which the Rayleigh line is tangent to the Hugoniot curve rather than crossing it. At tangency the two solutions of the previous screen merge into one, and the downstream flow is exactly sonic relative to the wave. That last statement is what gives the condition its physical meaning — a sonic downstream state cannot be influenced by anything behind it, so the wave becomes self-sustaining and independent of the boundary conditions that determine which branch an overdriven wave takes.

Derivation

Take the same intersection as the Hugoniot screen and impose that the discriminant of the quadratic vanishes. That is the algebraic statement of tangency. The discriminant is itself a quadratic in the group γ₁Ma₁², and setting it to zero gives the equation shown above, whose two roots are the two tangency points: the upper one on the detonation branch and the lower one on the deflagration branch.

Solving for γ₁Ma₁² rather than for Ma₁ directly is not cosmetic. That group is what appears in the Rayleigh slope, so the equation in it has constant coefficients built from the heat release and the two specific heat ratios; solving for Ma₁ instead would carry γ₁ through every term for no benefit. The Mach number follows by dividing and taking a root, and the wave speed follows by multiplying by the upstream sound speed, which is built from the gas constant and the upstream temperature.

The end state at each tangency point comes from substituting that Mach number back into the Rayleigh line, where the now-repeated root gives the volume and pressure ratios directly.

Assumptions

  • Everything the Hugoniot screen assumes: thin steady one-dimensional wave, ideal gas each side.
  • The heat release is known and is an input. In a real detonation it is set by the equilibrium composition at the end state, which depends on the very temperature being solved for — a coupling this screen does not close.
  • The wave is self-sustaining, which is what the tangency condition encodes. An overdriven detonation is not at the CJ point.

  • Whether a branch exists at all is settled by the heat release: with none, the two merge.

Where it stops

  • Zero or negative heat release, where the tangency condition degenerates.
  • Any case where the heat release is strongly coupled to the end-state temperature, which is most real detonations. The number here is only as good as the heat release you supplied.

How it is solved

Closed form. The tangency condition reduces to a quadratic in γ₁Ma₁², solved directly; each root gives one branch.

Not covered

  • The cell structure of a real detonation, its initiation, and its failure diameter — none of which appear in a one-dimensional steady treatment.

Reading the result

The CJ detonation speed is the quantity most often compared against measurement, and it usually agrees well — often within a few per cent — which is remarkable for a model containing no chemistry at all. That agreement rests entirely on the heat release you supplied being the right one. Supplying a heating value instead of the heat release per unit mass of mixture is the commonest error, and it inflates the answer substantially: the heating value is per unit mass of fuel, and a stoichiometric hydrocarbon-air mixture is mostly air.

The deflagration branch is a mathematical companion rather than a description of an ordinary flame. Real laminar flames propagate at a metre or two per second, far below even the CJ deflagration speed, and are governed by diffusion and chemistry rather than by this tangency condition. The CJ deflagration is an upper limit on a subsonic combustion wave, not a prediction of one.

Stated on the screen

Frozen composition: the heat release is an input, not derived from equilibrium.

von Neumann State

MOD-09

The state behind the leading shock, overdrive factor and induction length.

(p_N)/(p₁) = (2γ₁ Ma₁² - (γ₁ - 1))/(γ₁ + 1)

Basis

A detonation is not a single discontinuity. It is a leading shock followed by a reaction zone, and the gas reaches its peak pressure not at the end state but immediately behind that shock, before any heat has been released. That peak is the von Neumann state, and computing it requires nothing but the ordinary normal-shock relations applied to the unreacted gas.

Derivation

Apply mass, momentum and energy across a discontinuity with no heat addition — which is what the leading shock is, because the gas has not had time to react while crossing it. Eliminating velocity gives the standard normal-shock pressure ratio shown above, and the temperature ratio and downstream Mach number follow from the same three equations. The specific heat ratio used throughout is the unburned one, because the gas crossing the shock is still unburned; using the burned value here is a straightforward error that produces a plausible number.

The reason this state matters is what happens after it. The gas is now hot and compressed, the reaction begins, and as heat is released the state moves along the Rayleigh line — downward in pressure — from the von Neumann point to the Chapman–Jouguet point at the end of the reaction zone. So the pressure inside a detonation is highest at the front and falls through the reaction zone, which is the opposite of what a single-discontinuity picture suggests and is the reason structures exposed to detonations see a short pressure spike well above the CJ pressure.

Assumptions

  • The leading shock is frozen: no reaction occurs while the gas crosses it. This is good when the induction time is long compared with the shock thickness, which it normally is.
  • Ideal gas with the unburned specific heat ratio.
  • The shock is normal to the flow and steady in the wave frame.

Read this as the peak, not the end state. The sequence is: the gas crosses the leading shock and reaches the von Neumann pressure; it then reacts, and the pressure falls along the Rayleigh line to the Chapman–Jouguet value. If you are sizing something for a detonation load, the von Neumann pressure is the transient peak and the CJ pressure is what follows it — and a design based on the CJ value alone has underestimated the peak by roughly a factor of two.

The temperature behind the shock is what actually initiates the reaction, which is why it is reported. An ignition delay evaluated at the initial temperature rather than at this one will be wrong by orders of magnitude, because the delay depends exponentially on temperature and this temperature is several times the initial one.

Laminar Flames & Limits

A laminar flame is a balance between diffusion carrying heat and radicals forward and chemistry consuming reactants. Every screen in this family is a consequence of that balance being tipped one way or another: by activation energy, by heat loss to a wall, by stretch, by residence time. They share a single structural feature — a large activation energy, which makes the reaction rate change by orders of magnitude over a temperature range in which everything else barely moves. That is what produces thresholds rather than gradual transitions, and it is why so many of these screens report a critical value rather than a curve.

Laminar Flame Speed

MOD-10

Asymptotic solution, and scaling a measured flame speed to other conditions.

S_L ∝ p^((n-2)/2) e^(-T_a/(2T_f))

Basis

A premixed flame propagates because heat conducted forward from the reaction zone raises the unburned gas to a temperature at which it reacts. The speed is therefore set by a balance between thermal diffusion and reaction rate, and the square-root form that comes out of that balance is the reason flame speed scales as the square root of a diffusivity times a reaction rate rather than linearly in either.

Derivation

The large activation energy is what makes the problem tractable. Because the reaction rate depends exponentially on temperature, reaction is confined to a thin layer near the hottest part of the flame while the rest is a purely diffusive preheat zone with negligible reaction. Matching a diffusive solution in the preheat zone to a reactive one in the thin layer gives the eigenvalue of the problem, which is the mass burning flux, and dividing by the unburned density gives the speed.

What emerges is a product of three factors: a diffusivity, a reaction rate evaluated at the flame temperature, and the inverse of the Zeldovich number. The third factor is easy to overlook and is where the asymptotics actually enter — it is the measure of how thin the reaction zone is. Both the exponential and the Zeldovich factor are evaluated at the flame temperature, not at some intermediate temperature; that detail was confirmed character by character against a high-resolution rendering of the source, because an earlier reading had taken a subscript from an integration limit and attached it to the exponent.

The scaling route abandons the asymptotics entirely and keeps only the proportionalities. If the overall reaction is of order n, the reaction rate scales as pressure to the n, the diffusivity as pressure to the minus one, and the square root of their product gives the speed scaling as p to the (n−2)/2. The temperature dependence comes from the exponential alone. Neither route needs the other, and they answer different questions: one computes a speed from kinetics, the other moves a measurement to new conditions.

Assumptions

  • A single overall reaction with a large activation energy. Real chain-branching chemistry has no single activation energy, and fitting one is what makes the pre-exponential mixture-specific rather than universal.
  • One-dimensional, steady, planar, adiabatic flame. A stretched or curved flame is the subject of the flame stretch screen.
  • Constant properties across the flame, evaluated at the flame temperature. Density and conductivity vary by a factor of several across a real flame.
  • For the scaling route: the same overall order applies at both conditions. If the chemistry changes character between them — different limiting step — the exponent changes too.

Offering two as a convenient default would hand a reader a pressure-independent flame speed and no indication that they had been given one.

The mass burning flux, not the speed, is the intrinsic quantity. The speed depends on the unburned density and therefore on pressure and preheat temperature in ways that have nothing to do with the chemistry; the flux does not. When comparing two conditions, or applying the Lewis correction, work with the flux and convert at the end. This is also why the Lewis correction is applied to the flux here rather than to the speed, which is where some presentations put it.

Flame Scales

MOD-11

Zeldovich and Lewis numbers, preheat and reaction zone thickness, mass burning flux.

Ze = (Eₐ(T_b - Tᵤ))/(R° T_b²), Le = α/D

Basis

A flame has two length scales, not one, and almost every qualitative statement about flame behaviour is really a statement about their ratio. This screen computes both, together with the dimensionless groups that govern how the flame responds to being perturbed.

Derivation

The Zeldovich number measures how sharply the reaction rate responds to temperature near the flame temperature. Expand the exponential about the flame temperature: the leading coefficient of the expansion is the activation energy divided by the gas constant times the flame temperature squared, and multiplying by the temperature rise across the flame non-dimensionalises it. A value of order ten means the reaction rate falls by a factor of e for every tenth of the temperature rise you drop below the flame temperature, which is what confines the reaction to a thin layer.

The preheat thickness follows from the diffusive balance: a scale over which conduction can keep up with convection at the flame speed, which is the thermal diffusivity divided by the speed. The reaction zone is thinner than that by exactly the Zeldovich number, which is the same statement as before read as a length rather than as a sensitivity. For an ordinary hydrocarbon flame this puts the preheat zone at a fraction of a millimetre and the reaction zone at tens of microns.

The Lewis number is the ratio of the two diffusivities, and it decides whether a bulge in the flame front grows or decays: if heat diffuses away faster than reactant diffuses in, a bulge cools and flattens. The expansion ratio is the density ratio across the flame, which drives the hydrodynamic instability that acts in the opposite direction. Both are reported because the competition between them is what determines whether a flame is smooth or cellular.

Assumptions

  • A single overall activation energy, as on the flame speed screen.
  • Constant properties, evaluated at a single representative temperature. The factor of several variation across a real flame means these thicknesses are order-of-magnitude estimates rather than measurements.
  • The two-zone asymptotic structure, which requires the Zeldovich number to be large. At a value of three the two zones are not separated and the thicknesses reported stop meaning what they say.

Flammability Limits

MOD-12

Le Chatelier mixing rule and the Burgess-Wheeler limit temperature law.

(LFL)ₘᵢₓ = [Σᵢ Xᵢ/(LFL)ᵢ]⁻¹

Basis

A flammability limit is the composition at which a flame can no longer propagate away from an ignition source. It is not a thermodynamic property. It is the outcome of a competition between heat release and heat loss in a particular apparatus, and that is why two careful measurements of the same fuel can disagree — they were not measuring quite the same thing.

Derivation

Le Chatelier's rule treats each fuel in a blend as contributing independently, each consuming a fraction of the available flammability in proportion to how close it is to its own limit. Writing that statement and requiring the fractions to sum to one at the mixture limit gives the reciprocal-average form above. The rule has no derivation from first principles — it is an empirical regularity that works well for fuels of similar character and poorly for blends of very different ones.

The Burgess–Wheeler observation is different in kind: across many hydrocarbons the product of the lean limit and the heat of combustion is roughly constant, which says that the limit corresponds to a roughly fixed adiabatic temperature rise. That constant is the second input, and it is where this module ran into trouble worth recording.

The source consulted prints a constant of 920 together with the inputs it was derived from. Those inputs multiply to 960, not 920. The discrepancy is in the source, not in the transcription: 5.0 × 192 = 960 exactly. The relation is used here and the printed constant is not; the field defaults to the value that follows from the printed inputs, and a reader who wants the printed constant can type it.

Assumptions

  • Each fuel in the blend contributes independently at its own limit. Blends whose components interact chemically — inhibitors, in particular — violate this and the rule fails badly rather than gradually.
  • The mole fraction entered is among the fuels only, not in the whole mixture including air. This is the commonest error on this screen and it produces a plausible but low answer.
  • The component limits were measured under comparable conditions. Combining a limit measured in a 5 cm tube with one measured in a 2 inch tube mixes two different quantities.

Thermal Explosion

MOD-13

Semenov criterion, critical temperature rise, and Frank-Kamenetskii critical size.

Tᵢ - T₀ = R°T₀²/E, θ_ig = 1

Basis

Self-heating runs away when the heat a reacting body generates rises faster with temperature than the heat it can lose. Both sides of that comparison are curves, and ignition is the condition that they become tangent rather than crossing — beyond it there is no steady state left to sit in.

Derivation

Semenov's version assumes the interior is at a uniform temperature and loses heat through a surface coefficient. Generation is exponential in temperature; loss is linear. Requiring the two curves to be tangent gives two conditions — equal values and equal slopes — and eliminating between them gives the critical temperature rise above ambient, which turns out to depend only on the ambient temperature and the activation energy. Nothing about the size or the heat of reaction survives into that expression, which is what makes it so compact and so easy to misapply.

Frank-Kamenetskii's version drops the uniform-temperature assumption and solves the conduction equation inside the body with an exponential source. The exponential is approximated by expanding about the surface temperature — justified by the same large activation energy that appears everywhere in this family — which turns the problem into one with a known solution and a critical value of a single dimensionless group. That critical value depends on the shape: a slab, a cylinder and a sphere have values spanning nearly a factor of four, and the characteristic length means the half-thickness for a slab and the radius for the other two.

The two versions answer different questions and both are shown. Semenov says how much self-heating you can tolerate before runaway; Frank-Kamenetskii says how large a body of that material can be at that ambient temperature before it cannot lose heat fast enough.

Assumptions

  • Semenov: the Biot number is small, so the interior is nearly isothermal and all resistance is at the surface.
  • Frank-Kamenetskii: the Biot number is large, so the surface is at ambient and all resistance is internal. The two assumptions are opposite and the screen reports both regardless of which holds.
  • Zero-order kinetics: reactant consumption is neglected. A body that runs out of reactant before running away does not ignite, and this model would say it does.
  • The exponential approximation to the Arrhenius term, which requires a large activation energy.

Reading the result

The critical temperature rise is small — of order twenty or thirty kelvin for a typical activation energy at a few hundred kelvin — and that smallness is the whole point. Ignition is not a slow climb to a high temperature: the steady state is lost within that narrow rise, and after it there is no steady state to return to. A body observed to be twenty kelvin above ambient is not comfortably warm; it may be at the edge.

The critical size is a stability boundary, not a safe size. It is computed for a perfectly uniform body at a fixed ambient temperature with no reactant consumption and no convection, and every one of those idealisations moves the real boundary. Use it to understand how the boundary scales — with the square root of conductivity, inversely with the square root of the rate — rather than as a number to design a store to.

Stirred Reactor

MOD-14

The S-curve: ignition and extinction turning points of a well-stirred reactor.

Da(θ) = (θ)/(1-θ) e^(T_a/T_f)

Basis

A perfectly stirred reactor has one temperature and one composition throughout, so its steady states are the roots of a single algebraic balance between heat released by reaction and heat carried away by the through-flow. With a large activation energy that balance has either one root or three, and the transition between those two cases is what produces ignition and extinction as discrete events rather than gradual changes.

Derivation

Write the steady balance in terms of the reaction progress — the fraction of the adiabatic temperature rise actually achieved. Convection removes heat in proportion to that progress; reaction supplies it in proportion to the remaining reactant times an exponential in the reactor temperature, and the reactor temperature is itself a linear function of the progress. Rearranging gives the Damköhler number explicitly as a function of the progress, which is the relation shown above.

That rearrangement is the whole trick, and it is worth being explicit about why. The physically natural question is "given a Damköhler number, what is the temperature" — but posed that way the answer is multi-valued and the turning points are exactly where the curve doubles back, with a vertical tangent. A solver marching in Damköhler number would step straight past them. Posed the other way round, with progress as the independent variable, the relation is single-valued and smooth, and the turning points become ordinary interior extrema of a scalar function — a maximum and a minimum — which are found by scanning.

The S-curve on the screen is that relation plotted with the axes swapped back to the physically natural orientation, which is why it folds.

Assumptions

  • Perfect stirring: one temperature and one composition describe the whole reactor. A real reactor with a finite mixing time has neither.
  • A single overall reaction step with a large activation energy.
  • Adiabatic walls, so all heat leaves with the flow.
  • First-order consumption of a single deficient reactant.

Reading the result

The Damköhler numbers here span many decades and only their ratio carries meaning. The definition folds the pre-exponential factor into the group, so the absolute value depends on a kinetic constant that was never entered; comparing one of these numbers against a value from another source without checking that both used the same definition will produce nonsense.

With a one-step reaction and a cold inlet the ignition branch sits at an enormous Damköhler number, and that is not an artefact. Such a mixture really is essentially inert at the inlet temperature — a one-step Arrhenius rate at 300 K with a combustion activation energy is vanishingly small, so an absurdly long residence time would be needed to ignite it. Real mixtures ignite far earlier through chain branching, which builds radical pools at temperatures where the one-step rate is still negligible. The model is not wrong; it is answering a question about a mixture that does not chain-branch.

What the screen is genuinely good for is the hysteresis. Once burning, the reactor keeps burning over a range of residence times in which it could never have ignited, and that gap is why a combustor can be stable in operation yet hard to light.

Basis

A flame front that is curved or sitting in a straining flow has its area changing as it moves, and that changes the balance between the heat it loses sideways and the reactant it receives. Stretch is the rate of fractional area change, and to first order the flame speed responds to it linearly. The constant of proportionality is a length — the Markstein length — and its sign decides whether stretch helps or hinders.

Derivation

Expand the flame's response to a weak perturbation of the front. At leading order nothing changes; at first order in the stretch rate the speed shifts by an amount proportional to the stretch, with a coefficient that has units of length because stretch has units of inverse time. That coefficient combines the flame thickness with the Lewis number and the Zeldovich number, and for a mixture with a Lewis number below one it comes out negative — meaning positive stretch slows the flame.

The extinction condition comes from a different argument. Carrying the expansion further and asking where the response ceases to have a solution gives a critical stretch at which the flame can no longer be sustained, and at that point the speed ratio takes the value e to the minus one half. That number is a pure result of the asymptotics with no fitted content, which is why it is reported as its own row: it is a check on the model rather than an output of the inputs.

The Karlovitz number is the stretch rate non-dimensionalised by the flame's own timescale — thickness over speed. It is the natural measure of whether the perturbation is weak, and the linear response assumed above holds only while it is small.

Assumptions

  • The flame responds linearly to stretch, which requires the Karlovitz number to be small. At Karlovitz numbers approaching one the linear form has no justification left.
  • A single Markstein length characterises the response. Curvature and strain have different Markstein lengths in general, and this screen carries one.
  • Quasi-steady response: the flame adjusts to the stretch faster than the stretch changes.

Quenching Distance

MOD-20

Quenching distance, minimum ignition energy, and their pressure scaling.

Eₘᵢₙ ∝ d³, U · d ≈ constant, d ∝ 1/p

Basis

A flame propagating down a narrow passage loses heat to the walls. Below a certain passage width the loss exceeds what the reaction can replace and the flame dies. That width is the quenching distance, and the same balance sets the minimum energy a spark must deposit to create a kernel large enough to survive.

Derivation

Compare the heat generated in a slab of burning gas of thickness d against the heat conducted to the two walls bounding it. Generation scales with the volume and with the burning rate; loss scales with the area and with the temperature gradient, which itself scales inversely with d. Setting them equal gives a condition in which the product of burning velocity and distance appears as a group, divided by the thermal diffusivity. That group is a Peclet number, and quenching occurs below a roughly universal critical value of it.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, the product of burning velocity and quenching distance is roughly constant within a family of fuels, because the diffusivity varies little among them — which is why that product is reported as its own row and is worth sanity-checking against the ten-to-twelve range typical of hydrocarbons in air. Second, the quenching distance scales inversely with pressure, because the burning velocity is roughly pressure independent while the diffusivity scales inversely with density.

The minimum ignition energy follows from requiring the spark kernel to be at least the quenching distance across: the energy needed to heat a sphere of that diameter to flame temperature scales as the cube of the diameter. That cube is what makes ignition energy vary over orders of magnitude across fuels whose quenching distances differ by only a factor of three.

Assumptions

  • Quenching is set by conductive heat loss to the walls, not by radical destruction at them. For most hydrocarbons in metal tubes this is a reasonable division; for hydrogen and for some wall materials it is not.
  • The reference distance was measured at the reference pressure, which is one atmosphere here.
  • Properties are constant and evaluated somewhere between unburned and burned.

Mixture Fraction

MOD-16

Mixture fraction, its stoichiometric value, and the Burke-Schumann flame structure.

Zₛₜ = [1 + ν Y_F,1/Y_O_2,2]⁻¹

Basis

If every species diffuses at the same rate, then certain combinations of species mass fractions are neither created nor destroyed by reaction — they are conserved scalars. Normalising one of them so that it is one in the fuel stream and zero in the oxidiser stream gives the mixture fraction, and it turns a problem in many reacting species into a problem in one non-reacting variable.

Derivation

Take the fuel mass fraction and the oxygen mass fraction and form the combination that reaction leaves alone: fuel minus oxygen divided by the stoichiometric mass ratio. Reaction consumes the two in exactly that proportion, so the combination has no source term. Rescaling it to run from zero in the oxidiser stream to one in the fuel stream gives the mixture fraction.

The stoichiometric mixture fraction is then simply the value of that coordinate at which fuel and oxygen would be present in exactly the proportion reaction consumes them. Setting the conserved combination to the value it takes at stoichiometric and solving gives the expression above. For a hydrocarbon burning in air it comes out small — around 0.05 for methane — which is the quantitative statement that a diffusion flame sits close to the oxidiser side of the mixing layer.

The Burke–Schumann limit takes one further step: assume the chemistry is infinitely fast, so fuel and oxidiser cannot coexist anywhere. Reaction is then confined to the surface where the mixture fraction equals its stoichiometric value, and on either side of that surface one of the two reactants is exactly zero while the other varies linearly with the mixture fraction. That is why the two mass fractions this screen reports are piecewise linear, and why one of them is always exactly zero.

Assumptions

  • Equal diffusivities for all species. This is what makes a single conserved scalar sufficient, and it is the assumption most likely to fail — hydrogen diffuses several times faster than a hydrocarbon, and a hydrogen flame is not well described by one mixture fraction.
  • Two feed streams of fixed composition. Three streams need two conserved scalars.
  • For the Burke–Schumann results: infinitely fast chemistry confined to a surface.
  • Low Mach number, so pressure is uniform and the thermodynamic state depends on the mixture fraction alone.

Droplet Combustion

MOD-17

Transfer numbers, the square law rate constant, droplet lifetime and flame standoff.

r² = r₀² - K t, K = (2(λ/cₚ))/(ρ_ℓ)ln(1+B)

Basis

A droplet evaporating or burning in a quiescent atmosphere sets up a spherically symmetric field in which the outward flow of vapour and the inward conduction of heat balance at the surface. Solving that balance gives a surface regression rate that depends on the square of the radius, which is the d²-law: the square of the radius falls linearly with time.

Derivation

Write the steady conservation equations in spherical coordinates around the droplet. Mass flux is constant through every spherical shell, which means the velocity falls as the inverse square of radius. Substituting that into the energy equation and integrating from the surface out to infinity gives an expression in which the mass flow rate appears inside a logarithm.

The argument of that logarithm is one plus a dimensionless group — the transfer number — which collects everything about the driving potential for the process. In the evaporation case it is the sensible enthalpy available to heat the vapour, divided by the latent heat needed to make it: a measure of how much surplus heat there is beyond what vaporisation consumes. In the combustion case the numerator gains a term for the heat released by burning the oxidiser that diffuses in, which is much larger, so the transfer number is an order of magnitude greater and the droplet burns far faster than it would merely evaporate.

Differentiating the resulting surface regression rate gives the square law, with the rate constant shown above. The flame standoff ratio follows from asking where the oxidiser arriving from infinity meets the fuel leaving the surface in stoichiometric proportion; it depends on the transfer number and the ambient oxidiser fraction alone.

Assumptions

  • Quasi-steady gas phase: the gas field adjusts much faster than the droplet shrinks. Good except at the very start and the very end of the droplet's life.
  • Spherical symmetry and no relative velocity. Any convection breaks this and increases the rate substantially.
  • Constant gas-phase properties, evaluated somewhere between surface and ambient. The choice of where is a real source of scatter between published calculations.
  • The droplet surface sits at the boiling point, so the whole droplet is at a uniform temperature and no heat goes into warming it.
  • For combustion: infinitely fast chemistry at a spherical flame sheet.

Two numbers here are routinely misread. Turbulent Regimes

MOD-18

Where a turbulent flame falls on the regime diagram, and the four boundaries.

Re = Da² Ka², Ka_δ = δ² Ka

Basis

Turbulence supplies a range of eddy sizes and turnover times; a flame supplies one thickness and one time. Which regime a turbulent flame is in is decided by how those two sets of scales overlap, and the regime diagram is a map of that comparison in two dimensionless coordinates.

Derivation

The turbulent Reynolds number compares the largest eddy to the flame's own diffusive scale. The Damköhler number compares the largest eddy's turnover time to the flame time. The Karlovitz number compares the flame time to the *smallest* eddy's turnover time — the Kolmogorov time — and it is the one that matters most, because the smallest eddies are the ones that can penetrate the flame structure.

The three are not independent: assuming a Kolmogorov cascade fixes the relation between the small scales and the large ones, and the identity Re = Da²Ka² follows. Only two of the three carry information, which is why the diagram is two-dimensional.

The boundaries are then statements about where these groups cross one. At unit Karlovitz number the Kolmogorov eddies are the size of the flame thickness, and below that no eddy can enter the flame — the flamelet regime, where the turbulent flame is a wrinkled laminar one. The second boundary uses the inner layer thickness rather than the whole flame thickness: eddies can stir the preheat zone long before they can disturb the much thinner reaction layer, so the reaction zone survives well past unit Karlovitz number. That second boundary is at a Karlovitz number of one over the square of the inner layer fraction, which puts it two orders of magnitude higher for a typical value.

The Gibson scale is the eddy size whose turnover velocity equals the flame speed — the smallest eddy that can still wrinkle the front rather than being outrun by it.

Assumptions

  • A Kolmogorov inertial cascade, which requires a large turbulent Reynolds number.
  • Homogeneous isotropic turbulence characterised by one intensity and one length scale.
  • The flame thickness taken as the mass diffusivity divided by the flame speed. Other definitions differ by factors of several and shift every boundary accordingly.
  • The inner layer is a fixed fraction of the flame thickness, independent of conditions.

Transport Properties

ln X = A ln T + B/T + C/T² + D

Basis

Transport properties of a dilute gas follow from kinetic theory, but the collision integrals that theory requires are themselves fitted quantities. Rather than reconstruct them, this screen evaluates the fitted curves published alongside the thermodynamic data — the same source, the same provenance, and no additional assumptions beyond the fit itself.

Derivation

The fit is in the logarithm: the logarithm of the property is a linear combination of the logarithm of temperature, its reciprocal, its reciprocal squared, and a constant. Four coefficients per interval, several intervals per species, chosen so that the form is smooth and well behaved over a wide temperature range.

Evaluation is therefore a lookup of the interval containing the requested temperature followed by an arithmetic evaluation and an exponentiation. The only subtleties are at the boundaries: the file stores viscosity in micropoise and conductivity in microwatts per centimetre-kelvin, both of which are converted to SI here rather than passed through, and it writes exponents with a space where a sign would ordinarily be.

  • The fitted curves describe a pure species at low density. They carry no pressure dependence, which is correct for a dilute gas and wrong at high pressure.
